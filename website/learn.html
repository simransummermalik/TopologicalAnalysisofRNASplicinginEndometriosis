What are we measuring?

Start with RNA splicing

When a cell first copies a gene into RNA, that RNA includes sections called exons and introns. Splicing removes introns and joins selected exons. Different joining choices can make different finished RNAs from the same gene.

RNA sequencing gives us short reads. Some cross an exon-to-exon join, giving evidence for a splice junction. We count the reads supporting each observed junction.

Why normalize the counts?

One sample may have more sequencing reads than another. Splice Girl divides each junction count by the total support for that sample and gene. Counts of 100, 100 and 100 become relative weights of one third each. This describes usage within the observed gene graph; it does not remove every sequencing or coverage difference.

Where Hodge theory comes in

Hodge decomposition separates the graph’s edge signal into two parts:

  1. Gradient: the part that can be explained by assigning values to nodes and taking differences along edges.
  2. Cycle space: the part left over after that best node-based explanation. It can capture signal around graph loops or alternative paths.

The cycle fraction compares the squared size of the cycle-space part with the squared size of the original signal. It lies between zero and one. A tree-shaped graph has no cycle space, so its cycle fraction is zero.

A graph loop does not have to be a directed biological loop. The example above can have a nonzero cycle component even though none of its arrows points backward. Cycle fraction is not the percentage of circular RNA.

Why Rust?

Rust lets us build a reusable command-line program with input checks and automated tests. It handles the graphs and math. Separate tools prepare the sequencing evidence: Cleaver cleans reads, HISAT2 aligns them, and our converter counts eligible annotated junctions.

What can the study tell us?

The planned analysis compares four endometriosis and four control samples of proliferative-phase eutopic endometrium. It asks whether their graph measurements differ, and whether Hodge summaries add information beyond simpler counts and graph measurements.

The candidate genes come from genetic-risk and environmental-response evidence. The samples have no measured exposure histories, so those gene labels do not show that a donor was exposed. Neither a graph difference nor a small p-value establishes disease causation.

Our checked real CDH11 example is one control prefix, not a completed case/control study. The full biological comparison remains pending.
